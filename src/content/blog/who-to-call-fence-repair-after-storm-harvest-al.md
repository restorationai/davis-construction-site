## What should you do in the first hour after storm damage to your fence?

In the first hour, keep pets and kids away from the damaged section, prop up or move any panel that could fall further, and photograph the damage from several angles before you touch anything else. Those two steps protect people in the yard and give you the documentation an insurance adjuster will ask for later.

If a tree or large limb came down on the fence, do not try to cut it loose yourself if it is under tension or leaning against power lines. Wait for a crew with the right equipment. If the fence line runs near a utility easement and any wires are down or sagging, stay well back and call the power company before anyone approaches.

## Does homeowners insurance cover fence damage from a storm?

Most standard homeowners policies cover storm damage to a fence under the "other structures" portion of the policy, sometimes called Coverage B, which pays out as a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit minus your deductible. Coverage generally applies to sudden events like high wind or a falling tree, not to a fence that was already leaning, rotted, or deteriorating before the storm hit, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance).

If a tree, yours or a neighbor's, fell on your fence because of wind, most policies pay to remove the tree and repair the fence under that same other-structures coverage regardless of whose yard the tree came from, as long as a covered peril caused it to fall. Coverage details and deductibles vary by insurer, so pull your actual policy before you assume anything. ## Is a leaning post a quick repair, or does the whole section need rebuilding?

A single leaning post with the rest of the fence intact is usually a post reset: dig out the old footing, set a new post in fresh concrete, and reattach the existing panels. A section with three or more snapped pickets, a collapsed run, or posts sheared off at the base typically needs that whole section rebuilt rather than patched.

Before any new post hole goes in the ground, whoever does the work needs to call Alabama's one-call utility locating service to have buried lines marked first, which is required by law before digging, repair or new installation alike.
