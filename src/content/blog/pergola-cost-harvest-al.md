If you're pricing out a pergola for a backyard in one of Harvest's newer subdivisions off County Line Road or Jeff Road, you've probably already noticed the quotes don't look alike. One builder's $4,500 estimate and another's $11,000 bid can both be for a "10x12 pergola," and the difference usually comes down to material, footing depth, and whether the roof is fixed slats or motorized louvers. ## How much does a pergola cost in Harvest, AL?

Most homeowners in the Harvest and Madison County area pay between $3,500 and $26,000 for a pergola, with the typical mid-size wood or aluminum project landing in the $6,000-$14,000 range. Size and material are the two biggest cost drivers, with labor, footing depth, and roof style adding or subtracting from there.

| Size | Wood (cedar or pressure-treated) | Aluminum (powder-coated) | Louvered (motorized) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10x10 | $3,500-$6,500 | $6,000-$9,500 | $12,000-$18,000 |
| 12x16 | $6,000-$10,000 | $9,500-$14,000 | $18,000-$26,000 |
| 16x20 | $9,000-$15,000 | $14,000-$20,000 | $24,000-$35,000 |

Wood pergolas cost less upfront but need re-staining every two to three years in North Alabama's humid summers to keep checking and graying at bay. Aluminum and powder-coated steel cost more initially but hold up better against the swing between July humidity and winter freeze-thaw cycles, and they don't need refinishing. ## How much more does a louvered pergola cost than a standard one?

A motorized louvered pergola typically costs two to three times more than a fixed-roof wood or aluminum pergola of the same footprint, because of the moving hardware, motor, and weather sensors. A fixed 12x16 aluminum pergola might run $9,500-$14,000, while the louvered version of the same size runs $18,000-$26,000.

The extra cost buys adjustable aluminum slats that rotate open for sun or closed for shade and light rain, usually controlled by a wall switch or remote, sometimes with a built-in rain sensor that closes the louvers automatically. For Harvest's long, hot summers and frequent afternoon pop-up storms, that adjustability is the main selling point: you get shade control without giving up the option of full sun on a cooler fall afternoon. Motorized components also mean a modest annual maintenance check on the motor and drainage channels, which fixed pergolas don't need.

## Does building a pergola on an existing deck cost less than a standalone one?

Building a pergola on top of an existing deck usually costs less than a freestanding pergola on new ground, because the deck framing can sometimes carry the post loads without new footings. Savings typically run $1,000-$3,000 depending on whether the existing deck framing is rated for the added point loads.

The catch is that not every deck can take a pergola without reinforcement. A deck built to carry foot traffic and furniture isn't automatically engineered for four pergola posts bearing roof and wind load, especially with Alabama's spring wind gusts. Before attaching posts to deck framing, a contractor should check joist spacing and beam sizing against the load the pergola will add; sometimes that means sistering joists or adding a mid-span beam, which narrows the savings. If your deck was built more than a few years ago or you're not sure what it was engineered for, get that checked before assuming the on-deck price applies. ## Is a pergola or a covered patio cheaper?

A pergola is almost always cheaper than a solid-roof covered patio of the same footprint, because a pergola's open slats use less material and need a lighter structural frame than a roof built to shed rain. A 12x16 covered patio with a solid roof and gutters typically runs $12,000-$22,000, compared to $6,000-$14,000 for a comparable wood or aluminum pergola.

The tradeoff is function, not just price. A covered patio keeps you fully dry in a downpour and can be tied into the home's existing roofline. A pergola, even with louvers closed, isn't built to be a watertight roof system; it's built for filtered shade with airflow. Homeowners who want year-round outdoor living space in all weather usually lean toward a covered patio; homeowners who mainly want shade for a grill, dining table, or hot tub during Alabama's six warm months tend to pick a pergola and save the difference.

## Do I need a permit for a pergola in Madison County?

Most pergolas in unincorporated Madison County, which includes Harvest, need a building permit once they exceed a certain size or involve permanent footings, though exact thresholds and the review process can change. Because Harvest isn't inside Madison city limits, permitting runs through the Madison County Building Department rather than the City of Madison, so don't assume city rules apply.

On top of the county requirement, many Harvest subdivisions carry HOA architectural covenants that require separate approval for any permanent backyard structure, including pergolas, before construction starts. Skipping that step can mean a stop-work order from the HOA even after the county has signed off. Confirm current permit thresholds and your subdivision's covenant requirements before ordering materials; the [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org/) publishes general guidance on how local permitting typically works for residential structures like this if you want background before you call the county.

A licensed contractor who regularly pulls permits in Madison County can usually tell you within a day or two whether your planned size and footing depth will need a permit, which saves a wasted trip to the county office.

Getting a written, itemized quote that breaks out material, footings, and any electrical work for a louvered unit is the single best way to compare bids apples-to-apples once you know your size and material.
