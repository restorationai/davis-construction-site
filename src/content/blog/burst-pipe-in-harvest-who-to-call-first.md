## What should I do in the first 60 minutes after a pipe bursts?

Shut off your main water supply valve immediately. It is usually near your water meter, in the garage, or in a basement or crawlspace access point. Turning this one valve stops the flow faster than trying to find the exact break.

If water is pooling near outlets, switches, or your electrical panel, shut off power to that area at the breaker box, but only if you can reach it without stepping through standing water. If the panel itself is wet or you're unsure, leave it and call an electrician before touching anything.

Next, move what you can. Pull furniture legs onto foil or wood blocks, lift rugs, and get boxes or electronics off the floor. If you have a wet-dry shop vac, start pulling up standing water. Don't use a regular household vacuum on water. If it's safe, open a few windows and run ceiling fans or box fans to start air movement. None of this fixes the pipe, but it buys your floors and drywall time.

## Who do I call first: a plumber or a restoration company?

Call a licensed plumber first to physically stop the leak, since no amount of drying matters until the water source is shut off for good. At the same time, or right after, call a restoration company, because the clock on mold growth and swelling floors starts the moment the water hits your home, not when the plumber finishes.

These are two different jobs done by two different kinds of companies, and homeowners in Harvest often assume one company does both. A plumber repairs or replaces the burst section of pipe, pressure-tests the line, and confirms it's safe to turn the water back on. A restoration company extracts standing water, pulls out saturated materials that won't dry in place, sets up air movers and dehumidifiers, and monitors moisture levels in the walls and subfloor until they're back to a dry baseline.

## What happens in the first 24 hours after the water is shut off?

In the first 24 hours, a restoration crew should extract remaining standing water, remove any materials that can't be dried in place, and set up equipment to dry the rest. A pipe burst from a clean supply line usually starts as what the [IICRC](https://iicrc.org/s500/) classifies as Category 1 water, meaning it came from a sanitary source. That water does not stay clean once it sits on carpet, soaks into drywall, or sits for more than a day or two; it can degrade into a more contaminated category, which changes how materials are handled.

During this window, expect a technician to use a moisture meter to check how far the water traveled behind walls and under flooring, not just where it's visible. Baseboards may come off, a section of drywall may get cut out at the bottom of a wall (a "flood cut"), and wet insulation almost always gets replaced rather than dried. Air movers and a dehumidifier typically run for two to four days, sometimes longer depending on how much the subfloor or framing absorbed.

## Does homeowners insurance cover a burst pipe?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from a burst pipe, since it's treated differently than a slow leak you should have caught sooner. What's usually NOT covered: damage from a pipe that was clearly leaking for weeks or months before it failed, which insurers often classify as a maintenance issue rather than a sudden loss.

Document everything before cleanup starts: photos of standing water, the damaged pipe section if your plumber can show it to you, and receipts for any mitigation supplies. Most policies also require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage once the leak is found, which is part of why starting the drying process quickly matters for the claim, not just for the house.
